The problem

Interconnect billing settles what operators owe each other for calls that cross their networks, so a lost or double-counted record turns directly into a billing dispute. The pipeline ran on a legacy Java River framework, and the application around it was an ageing Struts and JSP web app on Tomcat 7: hard to change, hard to see into, and tied to old switch formats.

The pipeline

What we changed

  • A new application layer: an Angular 16 web app on a reusable component library and a Node.js API, replacing the legacy web app.
  • Billing operations: domestic, international incoming and international outgoing invoices with an approval step, scheduling and regeneration, plus re-rating for disputed or failed records.
  • Reporting: regulator reports, call volume by partner and route, completion ratios and dashboards for incoming and outgoing traffic.
  • Operability: live statistics and pause, resume and reload controls from the browser, and a watchdog that restarts the engine on known failure patterns.
  • The pipeline: rebuilt call-record processing on the River framework to parse 985 files a cycle, each carrying 30,000+ session records of 390 fields, about 3.5 GB a day, including a new switch’s record format and country-wise pulse rating.
  • The hardware: specified and configured the high-availability servers it runs on: 96 cores, 256 GB of RAM and 18 TB of SSD storage.
  • One codebase, two operators: client profiles let the same build serve two interconnect operators.

What running it taught me

A sustain contract is a product with a different shape: fewer features, more attention to database growth, capacity and the next change request. A small team that clearly owns the system keeps it boring, which is the goal for billing.
